Voice principles
Six rules that hold everywhere: site, app, email, ads and sales decks.
1. Lead with the next step. Say what happens now and who does it, before any reassurance.
    ◦ Do: "Your nurse has your message. She'll reply by 7:30 am."
    ◦ Don't: "Don't worry, we've got you covered every step of the way!"
2. Name the feeling once, then help. One honest line about how it feels is enough. Then be useful.
    ◦ Do: "Waiting is hard. Here's when your result usually comes."
    ◦ Don't: "We know how incredibly overwhelming and emotional this journey can be…"
3. Everyday word first, then the real word. Say what it is in plain words, then name it. Patients will hear the real word at the clinic, so they should recognize it.
    ◦ Do: "A hormone your growing follicles make (estradiol)."
    ◦ Don't: "Your hormone levels" or "your E2" with no explanation.
4. Be specific. Times, amounts, names and dates beat adjectives.
    ◦ Do: "Your next payment is $6,133, due Nov 1."
    ◦ Don't: "Stay on top of your finances with ease."
5. Write in the patient's words. Use the questions people actually ask, like "I missed a dose. What do I do?"
    ◦ Do: "Do I have to call in my numbers again?"
    ◦ Don't: "Streamlined cycle-monitoring data capture."
6. Say who decides. When something is clinical, say plainly that the nurse or doctor decides. That builds trust with patients and clinics alike.
    ◦ Do: "Your nurse decides what happens after a missed dose, so we've sent it to Maria."
    ◦ Don't: "Our AI will tell you what to do."
The kitchen-table test
Before anything ships, ask: would a smart friend follow this at the kitchen table, tired, after a hard appointment? It applies to guides first, then every page, email and screen.
1. Answer first. Each guide section opens with one plain "In short" sentence. On marketing pages, the headline and first line say the point.
2. One idea per sentence. About 20 words or fewer, roughly an 8th-grade reading level.
3. Everyday word first, medical word second. "How many are swimming forward (progressive motility)."
4. Every number gets a meaning. "About 1 in 10." "5 mL is about a teaspoon." Check the math on every comparison.
5. A comparison or picture when it helps. Never cute about something hard.
6. End with what it means for you, or what to ask next.
7. Simplify the words, never the facts. Keep every sourced caveat that changes the meaning.
8. Cut what the reader doesn't need to decide or ask.
9. Comfort without implying they should have known. Nobody learns this before they need it, so skip "that's not your fault" about not understanding. Just explain it.
The tone is conversational and comforting where it's needed, never unprofessional and never cute.
Donor conception. Write with the future donor-conceived person in mind. Before any logistics, cover telling your child, the limits of anonymity, half-siblings and family limits, open-ID donors and counseling. Avoid shopping language. Never assume why someone donated eggs, sperm or embryos: no "extra," "leftover" or "more than they needed."

Mechanics
• Names: the brand is "Pumpkin" everywhere people see it: site, app, emails, texts, social. The logo wordmark is lowercase "pumpkin". "The Pumpkin" is the legal company name only, for contracts and filings. The domain stays thepumpkin.ai. "Kinpath" stays internal.
• Headings: sentence case. Headlines can be two short sentences.
• Numbers: numerals for doses, dates, money and times ("8:00 pm," "$6,133," "day 6"). Units every time ("612 pg/mL").
• Punctuation: periods over exclamation points; none at all in hard moments. Recommendation: no em dashes, to match house style elsewhere.
• No emoji in product or marketing copy.
• Calls to action: a verb and the outcome ("Start your free record," "Book a demo"), never "Learn more" alone.
• Spanish: written by a medical translator from the same principles, never machine-translated without review.
• Example data: fictional names and clinics (Maya, Maria, Bayside), always labeled "Sample data." Example questions are labeled as written for illustration, never presented as testimonials.
Length on marketing pages: headlines up to 12 words, one supporting line up to 20, card text up to 8. If a section needs more, show it with a layout (timeline, icon grid, product snippet) instead of a paragraph. Detail lives on deeper pages.
